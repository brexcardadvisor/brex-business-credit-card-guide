## Week 1: The Evaluation — What Brex Actually Is

Before applying for anything, I spent the first week just understanding what we were looking at, because the terminology is confusing. People use [brex business credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card), [brex credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card), [brex corporate card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-corporate-card) and [brex corporate credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-corporate-card) almost interchangeably, and they are all talking about the same product: the Brex Card.

Here is the first thing every founder needs to understand: **the Brex Card is a charge card, not a revolving credit card.** There is no balance to carry month to month and no purchase APR to compare. The full balance is repaid on a set schedule — daily or monthly, depending on how your account qualifies. For us that was a feature (no debt spiral, no interest), but if your business genuinely needs to float purchases for several months, a conventional revolving card is the right tool and Brex is not.

The background helped me trust the model. The [brex company](https://brexcardadvisor.com) was founded in 2017 in San Francisco by Pedro Franceschi and Henrique Dubugras, and its founding insight was simple: underwrite the business, not the founder. Instead of a personal credit check and a personal guarantee, Brex looks at your company's cash, revenue, funding and spending patterns. In April 2026 the company became part of Capital One — the acquisition was completed on April 7, 2026 — and it continues to operate under its own brand.

One more clarification from week one, because I got this wrong myself: despite how often people search for [brex bank](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-account) or even the merged word brexbank, **Brex is not a bank.** It is a financial technology company, and its account services run through partner institutions. That distinction explains a lot about how the product is structured.

My week-one notes ended with a shortlist of questions: Do we qualify? What will our limit be? What does it actually cost? The next week answered the first one.

## Week 2: The Eligibility Check — Do We Even Qualify?

This is the week that saves you time, because Brex is not for everyone and the requirements are knowable in advance. The full breakdown lives on the [requirements page](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-requirements), but here is what we checked against, line by line:

**Business identity requirements**

- A U.S. Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS
- U.S. incorporation — C-corp, LLC or another incorporated entity
- U.S. operations and a physical U.S. address (a P.O. box or private mailbox does not count)
- Sole proprietors and unincorporated partnerships are **not** eligible — full stop

**Financial requirements**

- For monthly payments, funded startups generally need a minimum cash balance — Brex publishes $50,000 for venture-funded startups, with some exceptions
- For daily payments, qualification leans on funds held in the Brex business account and factors like equity investment or revenue above published thresholds
- Revenue-based and larger companies are assessed on financial statements

And the two facts that made the whole thing attractive to me as a founder: **no personal guarantee, and applying does not affect your personal credit score.** The underwriting is on the business. That is precisely why the [brex corporate card for startups](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-for-startups) became the default recommendation in founder circles — a company with real cash in the bank but a two-year history and a founder with a thin personal credit file can still qualify for a serious limit.

We passed every line of the checklist: incorporated, EIN in hand, physical address, cash balance comfortably above the monthly-payment threshold. If you are a sole proprietor reading this journal, this is where your Brex story ends and the alternatives begin — I will point you to the right comparison in the final entry.

## Week 3: The Application — The Full Step-by-Step Guide

Application week. I am reproducing the exact steps we followed, in order, because this is the part of the journal other founders have asked me about most.

### Step 1: Do the honest eligibility check first

Run the Week 2 checklist before you touch the application. Confirm incorporation, EIN, physical U.S. address, and which repayment path (daily or monthly) your cash or revenue profile fits. Ten minutes here prevents a declined application later.

### Step 2: Gather every document in one folder

We prepared:

- EIN and incorporation details (legal company name, state of incorporation)
- Physical U.S. business address, plus a supporting document (lease or bank statement) in case verification asks
- Details for beneficial owners and the controlling officer (names, dates of birth, ID) for the compliance check
- Recent business bank statements, or a secure connection to the business bank account
- Funding details for startups (amount raised, investors), or recent financial statements for revenue-based applicants

### Step 3: Start the application on the official site

Most people find the starting point by typing [brex com](https://brexcardadvisor.com) into a browser. Create the admin account with a work email you control long-term — this becomes the credential you use at the [brex login](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-login) every day afterwards, so do not use a temporary address.

### Step 4: Enter company information exactly as recorded

Legal business name, EIN, entity type and state, industry, business address, and a short description of what the company does. Match your incorporation records and bank details character for character; mismatches are the most common cause of verification delays.

### Step 5: Complete identity and compliance verification

Brex verifies the business and the people behind it — a standard Know-Your-Customer process. Beneficial owners and the controlling officer provide identification details. This is a regulatory check, **not** a personal credit check.

### Step 6: Connect the bank or submit financials

This is what sets your limit. Most applicants connect their primary business bank account during onboarding. If you plan to use daily payments, funding the Brex business account is part of this stage, because daily-payment limits are tied closely to the funds held there.

### Step 7: Confirm your repayment schedule

Daily payments collect cleared transactions each day, keeping the balance near zero. Monthly payments settle the full statement balance each cycle. Either way, remember the journal's week-one lesson: it is a charge card, so the full amount must be paid — there is no minimum-payment option.

### Step 8: Wait for the underwriting decision

Straightforward applications from clearly qualifying companies are typically decided quickly. If Brex asks for more information, send exactly what was requested — partial answers are the biggest self-inflicted delay we saw other founders hit.

### Step 9: Configure the account before issuing a single card

This is the step everyone wants to skip and nobody should:

1. Create spend limits and budgets by team, department or purpose (software, travel, marketing)
2. Set approval policies — who approves what, and above which amounts
3. Set receipt and memo rules so documentation is captured at the moment of purchase
4. Invite the finance team and assign admin, bookkeeper and employee roles
5. Connect the accounting software so transactions sync to the right chart of accounts

### Step 10: Issue cards and activate them

Issue physical cards for people who spend in person and virtual cards for subscriptions, vendors and online purchases. Every card carries its own limit within the company limit, so no employee ever has access to the whole line. Your [brex card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card) program is now live — ours was, by the end of week three.

## Week 4: First Cards, First Logins, First Surprises

Week four was about the daily experience. Every morning now starts the same way: the [brex login](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-login), then the dashboard. Our dedicated notes on the login process — two-factor authentication, mobile app access, and fixes for common sign-in problems — grew out of this week, because with a whole team signing in, you learn the edge cases fast.

Three surprises from the first week of live cards:

**Surprise one: the limit conversation changed completely.** Our company limit was calculated from our actual financial profile — connected bank balance, funds in the account, funding history — not from my personal credit file. The detailed mechanics are in the guide to the [brex card limit](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-limit), but the short version is: daily-payment limits track account funds closely, while monthly-payment limits weigh your overall financials. When our balance grows, our capacity grows with it. Our old bank card never did that.

**Surprise two: virtual cards ended a recurring headache.** Every SaaS subscription got its own virtual [brex business card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card). When a tool was cancelled, we killed its card and nothing else broke. When one card number was compromised, we replaced that one card — not the credentials on forty services.

**Surprise three: employees actually liked their cards.** Each person's [brexcard](https://brexcardadvisor.com) has a visible personal limit, a simple view of what is left, and a prompt to photograph the receipt right after each purchase. Compliance stopped being a monthly nagging exercise because the system asks at the moment it matters.

## Week 5: Money Week — What It Actually Costs

Halfway through the journal, I sat down and audited the real cost, because "no annual fee" marketing and actual total cost are different things. Here is the honest ledger, and the fuller version is on the [brex card fees](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-fees) page.

- **The card itself: $0 annual fee.** That part of the marketing is simply true.
- **The software is where tiers live.** The Essentials plan is $0 per user per month, and Premium is published at $12 per user per month, unlocking more advanced controls. A ten-person team on Premium is $120 a month — real money, but compare it honestly against the finance hours it replaces.
- **Foreign transactions:** international spending works, but currency conversion carries an FX markup of up to 3%. Our one contractor payment abroad made this concrete: on heavy foreign-currency spend, that markup is a genuine line item, not a footnote.
- **Interest: none, because there is nothing to revolve.** The flip side is discipline: paying in full, daily or monthly, is a hard operational deadline.

My week-five conclusion: for a team our size, the fee structure was not the deciding factor in either direction. The deciding factor turned out to be time, which is week six's entry.

## Week 6: The First Month-End Close — Where the Value Showed Up

This was the week I stopped evaluating and started believing. Month-end close with our old card used to mean two days of archaeology: a statement, a pile of missing receipts, and a Slack thread titled "who bought this??" repeated forty times.

With Brex, the close looked like this:

1. Transactions had arrived in real time all month, each already attached to a card, a person and a budget
2. Receipts and memos were captured at purchase time because the system prompted for them then — not because anyone chased them in week six
3. Out-of-policy attempts had been stopped or routed for approval *before* the money moved, so there was almost nothing to investigate
4. Coded transactions synced to our accounting software, and the close took hours, not days

This is what people actually mean when they talk about [brex value](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-rewards). The points program is real — spending earns a base reward with category multipliers under the rewards programs, and our [brex card rewards](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-rewards) notes cover earning and redemption properly — but the points are the smallest part of the value. The real return is the finance time you stop burning, the duplicate subscriptions you catch because every vendor sits on a visible dedicated card, and a limit that reflects your company's actual cash position. Points are a bonus on top of operational value, not a substitute for it.

The expense system is the reason. Policies are enforced before purchases, documentation is automatic, reimbursements for out-of-pocket spending run through the same platform, and bill pay and travel tie back to the same budgets. Brex is best understood as finance software with a card attached — not a card with an app attached. Brex also reports payment performance to business credit bureaus, so paying in full every cycle is quietly building our company's credit profile in the background.

## Week 7: The Business Account, and Settling the "Brex Bank" Question

By week seven, questions from our own team forced me to understand the account side properly. Half the team called it "the Brex bank account," which is understandable — searches like [brexbank](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-account) exist because the product *feels* like a bank account inside the platform. It is not one. Brex is a fintech company; the [brex business](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-account) account is provided through partner arrangements, not a Brex banking charter.

Why does the account matter? For daily-payment customers, it matters enormously: because daily-payment limits are tied primarily to funds held in the Brex business account, the account balance effectively drives what the team can spend. For monthly-payment customers like us, it is more of a visibility and convenience layer — funds, payments, cards, expenses, bills and travel visible under one login. Either way, think of Brex as the operating system for company money rather than a replacement for every banking relationship you have.

## Week 8: The Honest Verdict — Pros, Cons, and Who Should Skip It

Two months in, here is the balanced ledger I wish someone had handed me in week one. The full assessment is in the detailed [brex card review](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-review), but the journal version is this:

**What worked for us**

- No personal guarantee, and applying never touched my personal credit
- A limit based on our company's finances — dramatically more useful than our old personal-credit-based limit
- Physical, virtual and purchasing cards, each with granular spend controls
- Expense management, reimbursements, bill pay and travel in one platform
- A month-end close measured in hours
- Payments reported to business credit bureaus

**What did not work, or might not work for you**

- It is a charge card: balances are paid in full, daily or monthly. If you need revolving credit, look elsewhere
- Sole proprietors and unincorporated businesses cannot qualify at all
- The cash and revenue thresholds will exclude very small or pre-revenue companies
- The FX markup (up to 3%) adds up for heavy international spenders
- Advanced controls sit behind the paid per-user plan
- A two-person company may find the platform's depth is more than it needs

**If Brex is not for you**, the realistic options are traditional bank business cards (revolving credit, personal guarantee, minimal spend control), Ramp (the closest spend-management rival), Divvy-style budget platforms for smaller teams, and banking-first platforms like Mercury if the account matters more than the card. The side-by-side comparison of [brex alternatives](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-alternatives) walks through each of them properly.

Among all the [brex business credit cards](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card) we evaluated, Brex won for one reason that only became clear by week six: it treated our company's money like a system to be managed, not a balance to be borrowed against. For an incorporated, growing company that can pay in full, that is exactly right. For everyone else, this journal's week-two checklist will tell you quickly and honestly.

If you are starting your own version of this journal, start where we did: the honest eligibility check, documents in one folder, then the ten steps. Two months from now, your month-end close will tell you whether you chose well. Ours did.

One last note for founders reading this at the research stage, the way I was in week one: do not choose a card the way you would choose a piece of software, by counting features on a pricing page. Choose it the way you would choose a finance hire — by asking what your month-end will look like, who controls spending before it happens, and whether the limit grows when your company does. Those three questions decided it for us, and eight weeks of notes say they were the right ones.
