# Brex for Agencies and Creative Studios: Client Projects, Freelancer Spend and Clean Books

Running an agency means living inside other people's budgets. A single week can include ad spend for three clients, a freelance motion designer in another country, stock footage licences, a location scout's train tickets, catering for a shoot, and a producer charging props to whichever card happens to be in her wallet. Then month-end arrives, and someone has to untangle which charge belonged to which client, which costs are reimbursable, and which ones the studio quietly absorbed because nobody kept the receipt.

This guide is written specifically for agencies, design studios, production companies and creative shops. It explains how [brex](https://brexcardadvisor.com) works as a spend system for project-based businesses, how to structure one virtual card per client or project, how to pay contractors cleanly, how approval flows work for producers, and how to set the whole thing up step by step so your books stay clean without your team filling in spreadsheets at midnight.

## Why Agencies Outgrow Ordinary Business Cards

Most studios start with one or two traditional [brex business credit cards](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card) — or, more honestly, the founder's personal card and a promise to sort it out later. That works until it doesn't. The failure modes are always the same:

- **One shared card, twelve people's spending.** The card number lives in a shared note, gets used for everything, and the statement is a forensic puzzle.
- **Client money and studio money blended together.** You front £4,000 of media spend for a client, it lands on the same card as your rent, and your cash-flow picture is fiction until the client pays.
- **Freelancers paid through workarounds.** A contractor needs software for a two-week project, so someone shares card details over chat. That is a security incident waiting for a date.
- **Receipts reconstructed from memory.** Producers are brilliant at productions and terrible at photographing receipts in rental cars.

A [brex corporate card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-corporate-card) programme solves these problems structurally rather than behaviourally. Instead of asking people to be more careful, you give every client, project and person their own card with its own limit, and let the system attach the paperwork at the moment of purchase.

## The Core Idea: One Virtual Card per Client or Project

The single most valuable habit an agency can adopt on Brex is this: **issue a dedicated virtual card for every client engagement.** Not one card per employee — one card per project.

Here is why it works so well for studios:

- **Instant cost attribution.** Every charge on the "Client: Spring Campaign" card is, by definition, a Spring Campaign cost. No tagging discipline required from busy humans.
- **Hard budget ceilings.** If the client approved $8,000 of production spend, set the card limit to $8,000. Overspend becomes physically impossible instead of a month-end surprise.
- **Clean client reporting.** Export the card's transactions and you have a line-item backup for the client's invoice — every charge, date and merchant visible.
- **Instant shutoff.** Project wraps or a client goes quiet on payment? Freeze or close that one card. Nobody else's work is affected.
- **Vendor isolation.** Put recurring tools (stock libraries, font licences, project-specific SaaS) on the project card, so a cancelled engagement does not leave orphaned subscriptions on the studio's main card.

Physical cards still exist for people — producers on location, studio managers buying supplies — but the virtual-per-project layer is what turns a [brex business credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card) from a payment method into a bookkeeping system.

## Paying Freelancers and Contractors Without Sharing Card Numbers

Agencies run on freelancers: designers, editors, developers, photographers, copywriters. Paying them, and covering their project expenses, is where informal studios leak money and security.

Brex gives you cleaner options:

1. **Issue a temporary virtual card to the contractor** for the engagement, capped at the agreed expenses budget, expiring when the project ends. The retoucher buys the plugins she needs; you never see a reimbursement form; she never sees your main card number.
2. **Use spend limits per person.** A freelance producer might get a $2,500 limit for a shoot week — high enough to solve problems on set, low enough that a mistake is survivable.
3. **Separate fees from expenses.** The contractor's day rate goes through your normal accounts payable; their project purchases go on their project card. At invoice time there is no argument about which charges were expenses, because they are literally on a different rail.

This is also where the [brex value](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-rewards) shows up in practice: not in points, but in the hours your operations manager no longer spends chasing a freelancer for a missing receipt from three weeks ago.

## Eligibility First: Can Your Studio Actually Get Brex?

Before the setup steps, check the gate. Brex is not available to everyone, and agencies trip on the same three requirements:

- **Incorporation.** Your business must be incorporated (for example a U.S. C-corp or LLC). **Sole proprietors are NOT eligible** — a one-person studio trading under their own name cannot open Brex, full stop.
- **U.S. registration details.** You need a U.S. EIN, U.S. incorporation and a **physical U.S. address** (a P.O. box does not count).
- **Financial profile.** Limits and approval are based on company finances. For monthly payments, funded startups are expected to hold roughly $50,000 in cash; younger businesses with less cash may be routed to daily payments, where settled charges are collected from your balance each day.

## Step-by-Step: Setting Up Brex for an Agency or Studio

Here is the complete setup, in the order that saves you the most rework.

**Step 1: Confirm eligibility and gather your documents.**
Pull together your EIN letter, incorporation documents, physical U.S. business address, and identification for beneficial owners and the person who will administer the account. Having these ready turns approval from a drip-feed of emails into a single session.

**Step 2: Decide who owns the account internally.**
Pick one finance owner (often the studio manager or head of operations) and one backup admin. Everyone else gets a role, not ownership. This decision matters later when approval flows need an escalation point.

**Step 3: Start the application from the official site.**
Most people find the application by typing [brex com](https://brexcardadvisor.com) into a browser; some type the single word [brexcard](https://brexcardadvisor.com). Either way, begin the business application, enter your company details exactly as they appear on your incorporation records, and connect your primary business bank account so Brex can assess your cash position.

**Step 4: Choose daily or monthly payments — deliberately.**
This is a cash-flow decision, not a formality. Daily payments keep the balance near zero and suit studios whose client payments arrive unpredictably. Monthly payments give you a statement cycle that lines up neatly with monthly client invoicing, but require the stronger cash position mentioned above. Pick the rhythm that matches how your clients actually pay you.

**Step 5: Open the Brex business account if you will use it as your hub.**
The [brex business](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-account) account sits alongside the cards: client payments can land there, card balances are paid from there, and your finance owner sees cash and spend in one place. Studios that keep their main banking elsewhere can still run Brex cards, but the single-view convenience is the point of the account.

**Step 6: Design your card structure before issuing anything.**
Sketch it on paper first. A solid default for a studio:

- One virtual card per active client engagement
- One virtual card per internal cost centre (studio software, marketing, office)
- Physical cards only for people who spend in the real world (producers, studio manager)
- Temporary virtual cards for freelancers, created per project

**Step 7: Set spend limits that mirror real budgets.**
Translate each client's approved production budget into that card's limit. Set individual [brex card limit](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-limit) values for staff cards based on role: a junior designer does not need the same ceiling as your head of production. Limits can be raised in the dashboard when a project legitimately grows — the point is that raising them is a decision, not an accident.

**Step 8: Build approval flows for producers.**
Configure who can approve what: for example, the producer can self-approve purchases under $250 on their project card, the head of production approves up to $2,000, and anything above that routes to the finance owner. Set receipt and memo rules at the same time — require a receipt photo and a project note at the point of purchase, while the shoot is still fresh in everyone's mind.

**Step 9: Invite the team with the right roles.**
Admins see everything; employees see their own cards. Give bookkeepers view-and-export access so month-end does not depend on one person's login. Nobody outside finance needs the ability to raise limits.

**Step 10: Connect your accounting software.**
Sync Brex to your bookkeeping platform so transactions arrive already coded to the right project and category accounts. This single integration is what turns month-end reconciliation with clients from a two-day excavation into an export and a review.

**Step 11: Run one pilot project end to end.**
Take your next small engagement, run all of its spend through its project card, invoice the client from the exported transactions, and fix whatever friction appears. Then roll the structure out to every active client.

## Approval Flows That Match How Productions Actually Run

Generic approval policies fail on shoots because productions are fast, weird and occasionally urgent. Design your flows around real scenarios:

- **Pre-approved categories.** Stock assets, standard software and travel booked through policy should flow through without a human touching them. Approvals are for exceptions, not routine.
- **Thresholds by role, not by person.** Producers get one ceiling, heads of department a higher one, finance the highest. When someone is promoted, you change a role, not twelve cards.
- **Out-of-hours reality.** If a producer is on location at 6 a.m. and the equipment hire company needs a deposit, a limit increase request that waits until Monday is a failed system. Build an escalation path — a named approver who can raise a project card limit from their phone.
- **Client-funded exceptions.** When a client asks mid-project for an extra deliverable, the approval note becomes your paper trail: request on the card, approval in the system, charge attributed to that client. Scope creep stops being a favour and starts being a billable, documented decision.

## Separating Reimbursable Client Costs From Studio Costs

This is the section that pays for the whole system. In a studio, there are three kinds of spending, and mixing them is what makes books dirty:

1. **Studio overhead** — rent, core salaries, your own marketing. These belong on internal cards and never touch a client invoice.
2. **Reimbursable project costs** — media spend, freelancers hired for the engagement, travel for the client's shoot. These belong on the client project card, and they flow straight onto the invoice as documented pass-through costs.
3. **Project costs you absorb** — the mood-board printing you never charge for. These still belong on the project card, because you need to know what the engagement truly cost you to price the next one honestly.

When all three run through one card, an accountant rebuilds this split by hand every month. When each project card carries its own costs, the split exists from the moment of purchase — and your per-client profitability stops being an estimate.

## Month-End Reconciliation With Clients, Rebuilt

Here is the closing routine this structure enables:

1. **Export each project card's statement** for the period. Every transaction already carries its receipt and memo.
2. **Match against the client budget.** Approved budget versus actual spend, line by line. Variances are visible immediately, and each one has an approval trail behind it.
3. **Generate the invoice backup.** Attach the transaction export to the client invoice. Clients pay documented invoices faster — and dispute them less — because the evidence travels with the bill.
4. **Close or reset the project.** Finished engagement? Close the card. Continuing retainer? Reset the monthly limit and roll forward with a clean period.
5. **Review absorbed costs.** Look at what the studio covered without recharging. That number, tracked monthly, is one of the most honest pricing signals an agency has.

## Honest Limitations for Agencies

Brex is not the right answer for every creative business:

- **Sole traders and unincorporated freelancers cannot use it at all.** If your "studio" is legally just you, this door is closed until you incorporate.
- **Very small incorporated studios** with one client and three expenses a month will not feel the benefits enough to justify any new system.
- **Charge-card discipline is mandatory.** A studio riding client payment delays may find daily or monthly full repayment uncomfortable in thin months.
- **It replaces spend management, not your accountant.** Clean inputs make the accountant faster and cheaper; they do not eliminate the job.

If those limits rule you out, the [brex business credit cards](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card) landscape still has options — conventional revolving cards for tiny teams, and rival spend platforms for larger ones — compared in our alternatives guide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**We are a three-person incorporated studio. Is Brex overkill?**
Possibly not, if you run several client engagements at once. The per-project card structure pays off through attribution and client billing, not team size. If you have one client and a handful of monthly charges, a simple card is fine.

**Can a freelancer really be given a card for just one project?**
Yes — that is one of the strongest agency use cases. Issue a temporary virtual card with a limit matched to the agreed expenses, require receipts at purchase, and close the card when the engagement ends.

**How do client deposits and retainers interact with Brex?**
Client payments land in your business banking or your Brex business account; they are revenue, not card activity. What Brex tracks is the spend against each engagement, which is what your reconciliation and invoicing need.

**What happens if a producer hits a limit mid-shoot?**
An approver can raise that project card's limit from the dashboard or app in minutes, with the change logged. Compare that to the traditional fix — someone's personal card and a reimbursement claim.

**Does applying affect the founder's personal credit?**
No. Brex underwrites the business, states that applying does not affect personal credit scores, and does not require a personal guarantee.

**We work with clients outside the U.S. Can we still use Brex?**
Your company must meet the U.S. incorporation, EIN and physical-address requirements to open the account. Spending internationally is supported, with the FX markup noted above.
