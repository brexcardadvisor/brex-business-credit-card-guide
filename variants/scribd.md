## Chapter 1: Getting Oriented — What Brex Is

[Brex](https://brexcardadvisor.com) is a financial technology platform built around one core idea: a company's spending tools should be underwritten on the strength of the business itself, not on the personal credit history of the person who founded it. The [brex company](https://brexcardadvisor.com) was founded in 2017 in San Francisco by Pedro Franceschi and Henrique Dubugras, two founders who had previously built a payments business in Brazil and then discovered, after moving to the United States, that a well-funded young company could still be offered a trivially small card limit — or rejected outright — simply because the business had no borrowing history and the founders had no U.S. credit files.

The product they built in response became the [brex corporate card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-corporate-card): a business charge card whose limits are calculated from company cash, revenue, funding, and spending patterns. Around that card, Brex grew into a full spend-management platform that now includes a business account, expense management, bill pay, reimbursements, and travel booking, all managed from one dashboard.

**Ownership note.** In January 2026, Capital One announced an agreement to acquire Brex, and the acquisition was completed on April 7, 2026. Brex continues to operate under its own brand with its founding leadership in place, but it is now part of Capital One. Any review, forum post, or comparison written before April 2026 describes the pre-acquisition company; the platform and card program described in this manual reflect the current, post-acquisition operation.

**A note on names.** Search behavior around this product is messy, and the manual uses the terms the way readers actually use them. People type [brex com](https://brexcardadvisor.com) when they are looking for the official website, and others type the single compressed word [brexcard](https://brexcardadvisor.com) when they mean the card product. Still others search for "brexbank" or [brex bank](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-account) because they assume Brex is a bank. It is not. Brex is a financial technology company, not a bank, and it does not hold a banking charter. Its business-account services are delivered through partner arrangements. That distinction is not pedantry: it affects how the account is structured, how limits on daily-payment accounts are calculated, and what you should expect compared with a traditional bank account. Chapter 5 and the glossary return to this point.

**Who this platform is for.** Brex is built for incorporated businesses: funded startups, growing companies issuing cards to many employees, and finance teams that want budgets, approvals, and receipt rules enforced before money leaves the account rather than reconciled after. It is a poor fit for sole proprietors, who are not currently eligible, and for businesses whose main need is revolving credit they can carry for months. Keep both halves of that sentence in mind as you read the eligibility reference in Chapter 3.

## Chapter 2: The Card, Defined — Charge Card, Not Credit Card

Before any procedure in this manual makes sense, one definition must be fixed. Readers arrive at this product through many names — the [brex credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card), the [brex business card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card), the [brex corporate credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-corporate-card) — and most assume these are separate products. They are not. They are different search phrases for a single product: the [brex business credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card), which is, in precise technical terms, a **business charge card**.

The distinction controls everything downstream:

- **A revolving credit card** lets the cardholder carry a balance month to month and charges interest (a purchase APR) on the carried amount. A minimum payment keeps the account current.
- **A charge card** does not revolve. The full balance is repaid on a fixed schedule. With Brex, that schedule is either daily or monthly, depending on how the account qualifies. There is no purchase APR to compare because there is no borrowing-over-time feature, and there is no minimum-payment option.

Three consequences follow from charge-card status, and each one appears repeatedly in this manual:

1. **No personal guarantee.** Underwriting is performed at the company level. No founder or employee pledges personal assets behind the account.
2. **No personal credit check.** Brex states that applying does not affect the personal credit score of the applicant or of employees, because personal credit is not the basis of the decision.
3. **Payment in full is an operational deadline.** Because the balance cannot be carried, the repayment schedule — daily or monthly — must be treated as a hard finance-operations commitment, not a guideline.

If your company's cash-flow model depends on spreading a large purchase across several months of payments, record that requirement now: a charge card will not meet it, and the alternatives discussed in Chapter 8's closing notes and in the verdict material are the correct direction. For companies that can pay in full, the charge-card structure is what makes high, finance-based limits possible without personal liability.

## Chapter 3: Eligibility Reference

Use this chapter as a pre-application checklist. An application assembled against these requirements proceeds measurably faster than one that triggers follow-up documentation requests. The complete public breakdown also appears on the [brex card requirements](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-requirements) page.

### 3.1 Business identity requirements

All of the following must be true before an application is worth submitting:

- The business holds a U.S. Employer Identification Number (EIN) issued by the IRS.
- The business is incorporated in the United States (C-corporation, LLC, or another incorporated entity form).
- The business has U.S. operations and a physical U.S. address. A P.O. box or private mailbox service does not satisfy this requirement.
- The business is not a sole proprietorship and not an unincorporated partnership. Sole proprietors are not eligible under current program rules.

### 3.2 Financial requirements by repayment type

Financial thresholds differ according to the repayment schedule the account qualifies for. Treat the figures below as orientation values published by Brex, not as guarantees — meeting a threshold never guarantees approval, because Brex also reviews the business model, source of funds, financial health, and expected spending pattern.

| Repayment type | Primary basis of evaluation | Orientation threshold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily payments | Funds held in the Brex business account; equity investment; revenue | One common published path looks for annual revenue above $500,000; funded companies may qualify on investment |
| Monthly payments (funded startups) | Cash balance and funding history | Approximately $50,000 minimum cash balance for venture-funded startups, with some exceptions |
| Monthly payments (commercial/larger companies) | Revenue and financial statements | Assessed case by case from income statements, balance sheets, and cash-flow statements |

### 3.3 What is *not* evaluated

For the avoidance of doubt, the following are not part of Brex underwriting:

- The founder's personal credit score or personal credit history
- A personal guarantee from any owner or employee
- The personal borrowing history of cardholders who will receive employee cards

### 3.4 Eligibility self-test

Answer these five questions. A "no" on questions 1–3 means the business is not yet eligible; a "no" on 4 or 5 means choose the repayment expectation carefully before applying.

1. Is the business incorporated in the U.S. with an EIN?
2. Does the business operate from a physical U.S. address?
3. Is the business something other than a sole proprietorship?
4. Can the business document either meaningful cash (around $50,000 or more for funded startups), substantial revenue, or funds it will hold in a Brex business account?
5. Can the business reliably pay its full card balance daily or monthly, every cycle?

## Chapter 4: Application Procedure

This chapter is the manual's core procedure. Follow the steps in order. Steps 1–2 are preparation; steps 3–8 are the application itself; steps 9–10 convert an approved account into a working card program. Skipping step 9 is the most common implementation error new customers make.

### Step 1: Run the eligibility self-test

Work through section 3.4. If the business fails on entity type, EIN, or address, stop and resolve that first — submitting anyway produces a decline on the record and no usable information. If the business is a sole proprietorship, do not apply; consult the alternatives material instead.

### Step 2: Assemble the documentation pack

Gather the following into one folder before opening the application:

1. EIN confirmation and incorporation details: legal company name, entity type, state of incorporation.
2. The physical U.S. business address, plus a supporting document (lease, utility bill, or bank statement) in case address verification is requested.
3. Identity details for beneficial owners and the controlling officer: full legal name, date of birth, and government identification, for the compliance verification in step 5.
4. Recent business bank statements, or the credentials needed to connect the primary business bank account during onboarding.
5. Funding documentation if the applicant is a startup (amount raised, principal investors), or recent financial statements if the applicant is revenue-based.

### Step 3: Create the administrator account

Open the signup flow on the Brex website and create the admin account with a long-term work email and a strong password. This account is the one used at the [brex login](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-login) page for daily administration, so register it under an email the business controls permanently — not a contractor's address and not a temporary inbox.

### Step 4: Enter company information exactly as recorded

Provide the legal business name, EIN, entity type and state, industry, business address, and a plain-language description of what the company does. Enter each field exactly as it appears in incorporation and banking records. Name and address mismatches between the application, the bank account, and the incorporation documents are the single most common cause of avoidable verification delays.

### Step 5: Complete identity and compliance verification

Brex verifies the business and the people behind it under Know-Your-Customer rules. Beneficial owners and the controlling officer submit identification details at this stage. Note for the record: this is a regulatory identity check. It is not a personal credit inquiry, and Brex states it does not affect personal credit scores.

### Step 6: Connect financial visibility

To calculate a limit, Brex requires sight of company finances. Most applicants connect the primary business bank account securely during onboarding. Applicants pursuing monthly payments on a commercial profile may instead upload financial statements — income statement, balance sheet, and cash-flow statement. Applicants planning to use daily payments should note that funding the Brex business account happens at this stage, because daily-payment limits are tied primarily to funds held there.

### Step 7: Confirm the repayment schedule

The account is assigned daily or monthly payments according to qualification. Under daily payments, cleared transactions are collected from the linked funding source each day, holding the outstanding balance near zero. Under monthly payments, the full statement balance falls due each cycle. Whichever schedule is assigned, the obligation is identical in kind: payment in full. There is no partial-payment setting anywhere in the program.

### Step 8: Await the underwriting decision

Straightforward applications from clearly qualifying companies are typically decided quickly. Applications requiring additional documentation take longer, and the controlling variable is response quality: when Brex requests a document, send exactly that document, complete and legible. Partial or substitute responses restart the review clock more often than any other applicant behavior.

### Step 9: Configure controls before issuing any card

On approval, configure the account structure first. This ordering is deliberate: the platform's value comes from controls that exist before spending starts.

1. Create spend limits and budgets per team, department, or purpose (software, travel, marketing, operations).
2. Define approval policies: who approves what, and the amount thresholds that trigger each approver.
3. Set receipt and memo rules so documentation is captured at the point of purchase.
4. Invite the finance team and assign roles: admin, bookkeeper, employee.
5. Connect the accounting software and map categories to the chart of accounts.

### Step 10: Issue and activate cards

Issue physical cards to employees who spend in person, and virtual cards for online purchases, subscriptions, and vendor relationships. Issue each card against a specific spend limit — never against the company's whole line. Cardholders activate their cards, add virtual cards to mobile wallets where supported, and the program is live. Finance administrators should confirm on day one that transactions are appearing in the dashboard as expected; see Chapter 9 if they are not.

## Chapter 5: Card Types Reference

The platform issues three functional card types from one system. All three draw on the same company limit and the same control structure; they differ in purpose and risk profile. For the full product walk-through, see the [brex card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card) reference page.

### 5.1 Employee cards

Physical or virtual cards issued to named staff for everyday spending: travel, meals, fuel, supplies, client costs. Each employee card carries its own cardholder limit inside a team budget, so a junior employee's card never exposes the company's full capacity. Employee cards are the correct default for any recurring in-person spend.

### 5.2 Purchasing cards (P-cards)

Cards dedicated to operational and vendor spending: SaaS subscriptions, cloud infrastructure, advertising platforms, and supplier payments. Assigning each significant vendor its own purchasing card produces two operational benefits. First, continuity — a subscription survives staff departures because the credential belongs to the company, not to a person. Second, containment — if a card number is compromised, it can be frozen and replaced without disturbing any other vendor relationship or any employee's card.

### 5.3 Virtual cards

Number-only cards created instantly for a defined purpose, subscription, or ceiling. Virtual cards are the right instrument for one-off purchases, free-trial sign-ups that require a card, and any situation where the spending ceiling should be exactly the expected amount and nothing more. Creation and closure are both immediate, which makes virtual cards the lowest-risk way to extend the program to new use cases.

### 5.4 The business account, briefly

Because so many users conflate the two, this manual states it once more in reference form: the [brex business](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-account) account is the funds-holding side of the platform — the place company money sits, from which payments are made and to which card repayments are tied. For daily-payment customers it also functions as the primary limit driver. It is offered by a fintech platform through partner arrangements; it is not a deposit account at a bank named Brex, because no such bank exists.

## Chapter 6: Limits Reference

Limits in this program exist at two levels, and confusing them causes most internal spending disputes.

**Level 1 — the company limit.** The ceiling on total card spending across the whole account. It is calculated from business-level factors: connected bank balances, funds held in the Brex business account, revenue, capital raised, and financial statements where provided. The company limit is dynamic. As the underlying financial picture strengthens, capacity can grow; as cash position weakens, capacity can contract. Finance teams should therefore treat the company limit as a variable to be monitored, not a fixed entitlement set at approval.

**Level 2 — card and spend limits.** The allocation of the company ceiling to people, teams, budgets, and purposes. A company might hold a large overall limit while giving a salesperson a modest monthly employee-card limit, plus a separate temporary travel limit for a conference week. Level 2 limits are set by administrators (Chapter 4, step 9) and can be adjusted as plans change.

**Calculation differences by repayment type.** Daily-payment limits are tied closely to funds held in the Brex business account: broadly, available spend tracks available funds. Monthly-payment limits rest more heavily on the overall financial assessment — cash, revenue, funding, and statements. The worked mechanics, including examples at different balance levels, are set out in the [brex card limit](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-limit) guide.

**Practical guidance.**

- Review Level 2 allocations monthly; stale limits are the top cause of declined legitimate purchases.
- Raise travel limits before trips, not during them — limit changes are instant, but the request usually arrives when the traveler is already at a checkout.
- If the company limit contracts after a large withdrawal, that is the calculation working as designed; replenishing account funds is the corrective action.

## Chapter 7: Fees Reference

The fee structure has three layers: the card, the software, and transaction-level costs. Read all three before budgeting.

### 7.1 Card fees

The card itself carries a **$0 annual fee**. There is no purchase APR, because the charge-card structure does not permit carrying a balance (see Chapter 2).

### 7.2 Software plans

| Plan | Published price | Orientation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Essentials | $0 per user per month | Entry tier: core cards, limits, and expense tools for straightforward setups |
| Premium | $12 per user per month | Advanced controls, deeper policy and approval features, broader global support |

The correct tier depends on the complexity of the approval chains, policy structure, and geographic footprint. A ten-person company with one approver rarely needs Premium; a multi-entity company with layered approvals usually does. Confirm current plan contents before committing, since plan composition evolves.

### 7.3 Transaction-level costs

- **Foreign exchange.** International spending is supported. When a transaction requires currency conversion, Brex applies an FX markup of up to 3% per its published details. Companies with heavy foreign-currency spend should model this cost explicitly; on $10,000 of monthly converted spend, a 3% markup is $300.
- **Late payment.** There is no interest mechanism, but payment in full is mandatory, so a missed cycle is an account-standing problem rather than a finance charge. Treat the repayment date as a hard deadline in the finance calendar.

The maintained fee reference is the [brex card fees](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-fees) page.

## Chapter 8: Rewards Reference

The rewards program earns points on card spending: a base earn rate across spending, with category multipliers available under Brex's rewards programs in common business categories such as rideshare, travel, restaurants, and software. Points are typically redeemable toward travel, statement value, or transfers to partner programs, subject to plan and current program terms.

**Evaluating rewards correctly.** Points should be the last layer of the evaluation, not the first. When experienced finance teams assess [brex value](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-rewards), they are usually summing a wider ledger:

- Finance hours recovered from automated receipt collection and categorization, instead of month-end chasing.
- Out-of-policy spending that never occurs, because limits and policies block it at the point of purchase.
- Duplicate and forgotten subscriptions surfaced early, because every vendor sits on a visible dedicated card.
- A spending limit that reflects the company's actual cash position rather than a founder's personal credit file.

Points sit on top of that operational value. A program with marginally better points and materially worse controls is the more expensive choice for most growing companies. The earning structure, redemption options, and fit analysis are maintained in the [brex card rewards](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-rewards) guide.

**The startup case in particular.** The [brex corporate card for startups](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-for-startups) is the canonical deployment: a funded company that needs real limits immediately, cannot offer a personal guarantee it is comfortable with, and is hiring fast enough that ad-hoc expense handling breaks within a quarter. For that profile, the rewards question is almost always secondary to limit adequacy and control. Companies comparing across providers — including traditional bank cards that revolve, and spend platforms such as Ramp — should work through the structured comparison in the [brex alternatives](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-alternatives) review before deciding, and the bottom-line assessment in the full [brex card review](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-review).

## Chapter 9: Troubleshooting Login and Access Issues

This chapter covers the [brex login](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-login) process and the failure modes administrators and employees actually encounter. Work the procedures in order; each assumes the previous checks passed.

### 9.1 Standard sign-in procedure

1. Open the Brex website or the mobile app and choose sign in.
2. Enter the work email and password created at registration (Chapter 4, step 3).
3. Complete two-factor authentication if prompted — an authenticator prompt, a pushed approval, or single sign-on through the company identity provider, according to account configuration.
4. Confirm the dashboard loads with the correct company context; multi-entity users should verify the entity selector before acting on any figure.

### 9.2 Problem: password rejected

1. Confirm the email address is the one registered to the account — personal aliases and former-company addresses are the usual culprits.
2. Check caps lock and keyboard layout, then retype rather than pasting (pasted values can carry stray spaces).
3. Use the password reset flow; the reset message goes to the registered email. If it does not arrive within minutes, check spam and any email gateway quarantine before requesting another.
4. After a reset, sign in on one device first and confirm access before updating saved credentials on other devices.

### 9.3 Problem: two-factor authentication failing

1. If using an authenticator app, verify the device clock is set to automatic — time drift is the leading cause of rejected codes.
2. If using pushed approvals, confirm the phone has connectivity and notifications enabled for the app.
3. If the device itself is lost, stop and escalate to an account admin, who can re-issue 2FA enrollment. Do not attempt repeated code guesses; lockouts compound the delay.

### 9.4 Problem: employee cannot see their card or limit

1. Confirm the employee was invited under the same email they are signing in with.
2. In the admin dashboard, confirm a card was actually issued to that employee and that the card is active, not frozen.
3. Confirm the employee's spend limit is above zero and the parent budget is not exhausted — a zero remaining budget presents identically to a missing card.
4. If all three check out, have the employee sign out and back in to refresh entitlements, then re-test.

### 9.5 Problem: transactions not appearing in the dashboard

1. Allow for normal posting delay on very recent purchases, then refresh the expenses view.
2. Confirm the accounting connection is still authorized; expired integrations silently stop syncing in both directions.
3. Confirm no filter (date range, team, status) is hiding the transactions.

### 9.6 Escalation rule

If a sign-in problem survives sections 9.2–9.3 and the account is a company admin account, escalate to Brex support with the account email, the exact error text, and the timestamp of the last attempt. Precise error text resolves admin lockouts faster than any narrative description.

## Chapter 10: Glossary

**Brex business account** — The funds-holding account within the Brex platform, used to hold company money, make payments, and (for daily-payment customers) drive the card limit. Provided through partner arrangements; see "Fintech, not a bank."

**Charge card** — A payment card whose full balance must be repaid on a fixed schedule, with no option to revolve a balance or pay interest. The Brex card is a charge card, despite being widely searched as a credit card.

**Company limit** — The ceiling on total card spending across the entire Brex account, calculated from company financials and subject to change as those financials change.

**Daily payments** — A repayment schedule under which cleared transactions are collected from the funding source each day; limits on such accounts are tied primarily to funds in the Brex business account.

**EIN** — Employer Identification Number; the federal tax ID issued by the IRS, required for Brex eligibility.

**Fintech, not a bank** — The standing clarification of this manual: Brex is a financial technology company without a banking charter. Searches for "brexbank" refer to this same platform and its partner-provided account services.

**FX markup** — The percentage added to the exchange rate on transactions requiring currency conversion, published at up to 3%.

**KYC (Know Your Customer)** — The regulatory identity-verification process applied to the business and its beneficial owners during application; an identity check, not a credit check.

**Monthly payments** — A repayment schedule under which the full statement balance falls due each cycle; qualification rests on cash, revenue, funding, and financial statements.

**Personal guarantee** — A pledge of personal assets behind a business obligation. The Brex program does not require one.

**Purchasing card (P-card)** — A card dedicated to vendor and operational spending such as subscriptions, cloud services, and advertising.

**Spend limit** — An administrator-set ceiling applied to a card, person, team, or budget inside the company limit.

**Underwriting** — The company-level evaluation of cash, revenue, funding, and financial health that determines approval and limits, performed without reference to personal credit.
