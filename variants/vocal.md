# The Month Our Startup Outgrew Its Bank Card: How We Moved to Brex Step by Step

The card declined at 9:47 p.m., somewhere between the coffee arriving and the waiter bringing the check.

It was not a huge dinner. Four people: me, my co-founder, and two people from a logistics company we had been courting for five months — the kind of client whose annual contract would have doubled our revenue. And the bank card I had been issued, the one tied to my personal credit file because that is how small-business cards work, simply said no.

I smiled, excused myself, paid with a personal card, and spent the walk home doing math in my head. The math was simple: we had outgrown the way we paid for things, and it was starting to cost us more than money. It was costing us dignity, time, and — if that dinner had gone differently — clients.

This is the story of the month we fixed it. It is also a practical guide: what we evaluated, the exact steps we followed to apply and set up our new system, what the first 90 days actually looked like, and the downsides nobody puts in the brochure. If your company is hitting the same wall, I hope our month saves you a few of yours.

## The Second Embarrassment: My Personal Limit, Eaten by Ad Spend

The dinner was embarrassing. What happened the following week was worse, because it was operational.

Our growth lead had scaled a paid campaign that was finally working — cost per signup dropping, demo bookings climbing. What neither of us had fully registered was that the ad platforms were charging the same bank card I used for travel, software, and the occasional team lunch. The card's limit was based on my personal credit history, set back when the company was two people and a landing page. By the second week of the campaign, the card was effectively maxed out. Charges started bouncing. A cloud hosting payment failed at 3 a.m. and paged our engineer. A design tool subscription lapsed mid-sprint.

Nothing about our business was unhealthy. Our bank balance was fine. Our revenue was growing. The problem was structural: a traditional business card underwrites *the founder*, not the company. It looks at your personal credit score, asks for a personal guarantee, and hands a fast-growing company a limit sized for a person's spending habits. Every purchase the team made was quietly competing with every other purchase, on one card, under one ceiling, with my personal credit on the line for all of it.

We tried the obvious fixes first. We asked the bank for a limit increase and got a small one, after paperwork, weeks later. We split spending across two cards and created a spreadsheet to track which card paid for what — a spreadsheet that was wrong within a fortnight. By the end of that month, I was spending Friday afternoons reconciling receipts from a shared inbox, which is a genuinely absurd use of a founder's time.

So we started searching in earnest for a [brex business credit card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card) alternative — although, as I would learn, the thing we actually needed is not technically a credit card at all.

We modelled both. Essentials covered our needs at the start; Premium's approval workflows and deeper policy controls were tempting but not yet necessary at fourteen people. **Fourth, rewards versus value.** Brex points exist, and the [brex value](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-rewards) breakdown is worth reading — points on spend, boosted categories, redemption options. But our CFO kept repeating a sentence I now repeat to other founders: the rewards are the rebate, not the reason. The reason is control — per-employee cards, per-card limits, receipts captured at the point of purchase, no shared card number floating around in screenshots. **Fifth, the alternatives.** We did compare. ## The Switch, Step by Step

Here is the exact sequence we followed, in the order we followed it. If you are planning your own move to a [brex corporate card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-corporate-card), I would not change a single step — the order is doing quiet work, especially steps 1 and 7.

**Step 1: Confirm eligibility before you dream.** Check the basics against the requirements: U.S. incorporation, an EIN, a physical U.S. address, and real operating funds. If you are a funded startup, have your latest bank balance and funding details handy; the roughly $50,000 cash level is the reference point for monthly payment terms. Sole proprietor? Stop here and pick a different product — this is the step where honesty saves you a rejection letter.

**Step 2: Decide who owns the account internally.** One person should be the account owner and admin — in our case, me, with our fractional CFO as a second admin. Decide this *before* applying, because permissions, approval chains, and card issuance all flow from it. A corporate card program with no named owner becomes a group project, and group projects are where receipts go to die.

**Step 3: Gather your documents in one sitting.** You will need your incorporation details, EIN documentation, company address proof, and identification for the company's controllers/beneficial owners. We put everything in one shared folder the night before. Ten minutes of gathering saved us the usual week of "can you send me that PDF again."

**Step 4: Submit the application.** The application itself is an online flow — company details, ownership details, your business bank connection. Because Brex underwrites the business, expect to connect your business bank account so it can see real cash and transaction history. There is no personal credit check and no personal guarantee, which still felt faintly illegal the first time I read it, having signed my personal name to everything since incorporation.

**Step 5: Choose your payment cadence.** Depending on your profile, balances are paid in full either daily or monthly. Funded startups with sufficient cash typically land on monthly terms; others may start on daily payments drawn from the connected account. Understand which one you are getting, because it changes your cash rhythm. Daily payments demand a healthy, stable balance. Monthly payments feel closer to a traditional card cycle — without the option to revolve.

**Step 6: Set up the account structure before issuing a single card.** This is the step everyone skips and later regrets skipping. In the dashboard, we created our departments, set per-employee spending limits, defined categories, and wrote simple policies: receipts required above a set amount, pre-approval for travel, software purchases routed through one admin. Twenty minutes of configuration, in exchange for never moderating a "can I buy this?" thread again.

**Step 7: Log in, explore, and learn the dashboard with fake urgency.** Before any real money moved, I spent one evening just clicking: the [brex login](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-login) flow, the dashboard layout, where cards are issued, where receipts land, how approvals are requested and granted, where the statements live. If you administer company money, an hour of low-stakes exploration beats a month of learning under deadline. Write down the three things your team will ask you most — for us: "how do I get a card," "where do I upload receipts," "who approves this" — and learn those cold.

**Step 8: Issue cards in waves, not all at once.** Wave one was the three people who spent the most: me, my co-founder, our growth lead. Virtual cards first for online spend, physical cards requested for travel and in-person costs. Each person's [brex business card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-business-credit-card) carried its own limit, sized to their actual role — which meant the growth lead finally had ad-spend headroom that had nothing to do with my personal credit file, and my card could go back to buying coffee without declining at dinners.

**Step 9: Migrate the recurring charges deliberately.** We listed every subscription on the old card — cloud hosting, analytics, design tools, the CRM, the ad platforms — and moved them one by one over a single week, updating billing details and watching each first charge land. Do not rush this step and do not skip the list. The subscription you forget is always the one that renews during a launch.

**Step 10: Retire the old card last, not first.** Only after two clean billing cycles on Brex — every subscription moved, every team member active, statements reconciling — did we close the old bank card. Keeping it as a dormant backup during the transition cost us nothing and removed all the fear from the switch. Then we closed it, and I will admit I felt a small, petty joy doing so.

## The Honest Downsides

Any article that only lists advantages is an advertisement wearing a trench coat. Here is what genuinely grated, and what you should weigh before switching.

**It is a charge card, and that demands discipline.** The balance is paid in full, daily or monthly. There is no option to stretch a big purchase across a quarter. For a company with lumpy cash flow, that is a real constraint, not a footnote. We treat the cadence as a feature — it forces the spending conversation to happen before the purchase — but a business that *needs* revolving credit should not pretend Brex is something it is not.

**The limit breathes both ways.** A limit indexed to your cash position grows when you grow — and shrinks when your balance does. In a tough quarter, your card capacity contracts at exactly the moment money is tightest. That is rational underwriting and uncomfortable operations at the same time. Keep other payment rails alive for genuine emergencies; we still hold a basic company account elsewhere for redundancy.

**International spending costs more than the headline suggests.** That FX markup of up to 3% is easy to skim past during signup and annoying to rediscover on a statement. If a large share of your spend is in foreign currencies, model it properly — it can outweigh the rewards you earn on the same transactions.

**The good controls cost money at scale.** Essentials at $0 per user per month is genuinely usable, and the card itself has a $0 annual fee. But as headcount grows, the Premium tier at $12 per user per month — the one with the advanced approvals and policies — becomes a real line item. Fourteen users is a rounding error; two hundred users is a budget conversation. Neither is a dealbreaker, but budget for the tier you will actually need in eighteen months, not the one that flatters this quarter.

**It will not take everyone.** Sole proprietors are excluded, and thinly capitalized companies may not clear the cash thresholds for monthly terms. Brex is built for incorporated, funded, or revenue-generating companies. If that is not you yet, the honest answer is to grow into it rather than apply hopefully and collect a rejection.

**It is not a bank.** I will say it once more because I got it wrong initially: there is no brexbank with a vault and a lobby. Brex is a fintech platform working with partner institutions. Practically, everything worked smoothly for us — but if your company requires a specific banking relationship, charter, or in-person service model, understand the structure before you move operating funds.

## Lessons for Other Founders

If I could send five sentences back to the version of me standing in that restaurant, wallet open, smiling through a decline, they would be these.

**One: your payment system is a growth system.** We treated cards as back-office plumbing until the plumbing started rejecting revenue activity — ad campaigns, client dinners, infrastructure. The moment spending touches growth, it deserves the same seriousness as your product stack.

**Two: separate the company's credit from your own.** A personal guarantee feels harmless at incorporation and becomes a genuine liability at scale — your limit, your credit file, your name on the company's burn. Moving to company-based underwriting was worth it for this reason alone; everything else was compounding interest on a good decision.

**Three: configure before you issue.** Limits, policies, and approvals set on day one are invisible infrastructure. Set on day ninety, they are an argument. The twenty minutes we spent in setup before the first card shipped saved at least twenty meetings.

**Four: migrate in waves, retire in last place.** New card first, subscriptions moved deliberately, old card closed only after two clean cycles. Boring sequencing is what makes a finance migration feel uneventful, and uneventful is the highest praise finance migrations can earn.

**Five: count control, not just points.** Points are a rebate; control is a system. The duplicate subscription and the out-of-policy expense our setup caught in month two were worth more than a year of rewards points — and they were the kind of value that never appears in a signup bonus comparison table.
