## Why Remote Teams Break Traditional Business Cards

Traditional business cards were designed for a company where everyone sits in one office, in one country, spending one currency. Distributed teams violate every one of those assumptions:

- **One shared card gets passed around.** The card number ends up in a shared doc or chat thread, which is a security incident waiting to happen.
- **Employees front their own money.** A contractor in another country pays for a tool out of pocket, then waits weeks for reimbursement across borders — sometimes losing money to conversion fees twice.
- **Approvals stall across time zones.** An expense submitted at 9 a.m. in Manila waits for a manager in California who is asleep. By the time it is approved, the early-bird price is gone.
- **Foreign transactions pile up fees.** Every SaaS renewal, ad payment and flight booked in another currency adds conversion costs nobody budgeted for.
- **Nobody sees spend until the statement arrives.** Finance finds out about a new subscription a month after it started.

A card program built for this reality has to be virtual-first, limit-based, asynchronous and visible in real time. That is the design philosophy behind the [brex corporate card](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-corporate-card), and it is why remote-first companies were among its earliest adopters.

## Virtual Cards First: The Right Default for a Distributed Team

Physical cards make sense for a traveling salesperson. For a software engineer in Warsaw who needs to pay for a cloud instance, they make no sense at all. Brex lets you issue virtual cards instantly from the dashboard, and for remote teams this should be the default for almost everything:

- **One card per vendor.** Give each SaaS tool, ad platform and subscription its own virtual card. If a vendor is breached or a card number leaks, you cancel one card — not the card half the company uses.
- **One card per employee.** Each remote team member gets their own virtual card with their own limit, so spend is attributable to a person, not a department's shared plastic.
- **Instant issuance, anywhere.** A new hire on another continent can be spending — within policy — on their first day. No courier, no customs delay, no card stuck in a mailbox in a country they left last month.
- **Kill switches that actually work.** When a contractor's engagement ends, their card is frozen or deleted in seconds from the same dashboard, wherever you are.

Physical cards still have a role: in-person travel, conferences and client dinners. Brex supports those too, and both types draw on the same budgets and appear in the same real-time feed. ## Multi-Currency Spending and the FX Markup: The Honest Numbers

A distributed team spends in whatever currency each vendor charges: USD for most SaaS, EUR for European tools and flights, GBP, AUD, PHP and a dozen others across the year. Two things determine what that actually costs you on Brex.

First, card acceptance is global — the card works internationally, which is the baseline requirement for any team spread across borders. Second, when a purchase requires a currency conversion, Brex applies a foreign-exchange markup of up to 3% on the converted amount. That number matters, and you should budget for it honestly rather than discovering it on a statement:

- Put high-volume foreign-currency vendors on a short list and estimate the annual markup cost — 3% of a $200,000 annual foreign spend is $6,000, which may justify negotiating USD billing with those vendors instead.
- Prefer vendors who bill in your home currency when the price is genuinely equivalent.
- Keep the markup in perspective: it replaces the personal-card conversion fees, reimbursement spreads and transfer charges your team would otherwise absorb invisibly.

## Timezone-Proof Approvals: Policies That Work While You Sleep

The deepest problem in distributed finance is not currency — it is latency. A purchase request that needs a human in another hemisphere to wake up before it can proceed will always be slow. Brex's answer is to move the decision *before* the purchase, into policy:

- **Spend limits are pre-approval.** When you assign an employee a card with a $500 monthly limit for software, you have already approved every purchase under that limit. No chat message, no waiting, no timezone math.
- **Budgets by team, project or purpose.** A marketing budget, an engineering-tools budget and a travel budget each carry their own limits and owners — and owners can be in different regions, approving their own queues in their own working hours.
- **Receipt and memo rules run automatically.** Instead of chasing people across time zones for documentation, you require a receipt upload and memo at the point of purchase. The expense arrives in finance already documented.
- **Exceptions route, they don't block.** Anything outside policy is flagged for review, and the reviewer sees it in their dashboard whenever their day starts. The employee is not stuck mid-purchase at 2 a.m. wondering whether to proceed — the rule told them in advance.

This asynchronous design is where much of the real [brex value](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-rewards) sits for remote companies: fewer stalled purchases, fewer out-of-pocket reimbursements, and a finance close that does not depend on everyone being online simultaneously. Rewards points exist too — spending earns points with category multipliers under Brex's rewards programs — but for distributed teams the operational savings usually dwarf the points.

## Who Can Actually Get Brex? Eligibility for Distributed Companies

Here is the part many remote teams get wrong: your *employees* can be anywhere, but your *company* must be anchored in the United States. The requirements (full detail on our [brex card requirements](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-requirements) page) are:

- **U.S. incorporation** — the company must be incorporated in the United States (for example, a Delaware C-corp or a U.S. LLC).
- **A U.S. EIN** issued by the IRS.
- **A physical U.S. address** — a registered agent address or P.O. box alone does not satisfy this; Brex requires a real physical address for the business.
- **Financial qualification.** For monthly payments, funded startups generally need around $50,000 in cash; other paths are assessed on revenue and financial statements. Daily-payment accounts are tied closely to funds held in the Brex business account.
- **Sole proprietors are NOT eligible.** If you operate as a sole proprietor — even a U.S.-based one — Brex will not approve the application.

The good news for distributed founders: there is no requirement that the founder live in the U.S., and no founder's personal credit history enters the decision. ## Step by Step: Applying for Brex as a Distributed Company

Follow these steps in order — doing the preparation first is what makes approval fast.

**Step 1: Confirm your entity qualifies.** Check the list above: U.S. incorporation, EIN, physical U.S. address, incorporated (not a sole proprietorship). If any item fails, fix that first or choose an alternative.

**Step 2: Gather documents in one shared folder.** You will need incorporation details, the EIN, the physical U.S. business address (plus a supporting document such as a lease, utility bill or bank statement if verification is requested), identification details for beneficial owners and the controlling officer, and financial evidence — recent bank statements or a bank connection, plus funding details for startups or financial statements for revenue-based applicants.

**Step 3: Start the application and create the admin account.** Apply with a work email on an account you will keep long-term. This becomes the master login your finance function depends on, so use a role-based or founder-controlled email, not a departing employee's inbox.

**Step 4: Enter company information exactly as registered.** Legal name, EIN, entity type, state of incorporation, industry and address must match your official records character for character. Mismatches are the most common cause of verification delays — and they are entirely avoidable.

**Step 5: Complete identity verification.** Beneficial owners and the controlling officer provide identification for the compliance (KYC) check. Remember: this is an identity check, not a credit check. Applying does not affect anyone's personal credit score, and no one signs a personal guarantee.

**Step 6: Connect your bank or submit financials.** Link the primary business bank account, or upload financial statements if asked. If you intend to use daily payments, plan to fund the Brex business account, because your daily spending capacity is primarily tied to the balance held there.

**Step 7: Confirm your repayment schedule.** Your approval will specify daily or monthly payments. Both mean paying in full — daily deducts cleared transactions each day; monthly settles the full statement balance each cycle. Choose your cash-flow processes accordingly.

**Step 8: Wait for underwriting, and answer requests completely.** If Brex asks for an additional document, send exactly that document, in full. Partial responses are the biggest self-inflicted delay in the whole process.

Approval in hand, do not issue cards yet. The rollout sequence below is what separates a controlled program from a expensive free-for-all.

## Step by Step: Rolling Brex Out to a Remote Team

**Step 1: Design budgets around how the team actually works.** Create budgets per team (engineering, marketing, support), per purpose (software, travel, contractors' tools) and per region if spending patterns differ. Name them clearly — a budget called "EU Travel" beats "Budget 7" at month-end.

**Step 2: Set spend limits per person and per card.** Decide a default monthly limit for a standard employee card, higher limits for team leads, and single-purpose limits for vendor cards. Every limit is a pre-approval, so set them at the level where you genuinely don't need to be asked.

**Step 3: Write the receipt and memo policy before anyone spends.** Require a photo or upload of the receipt and a one-line memo for purchases above a threshold you choose. Publish the rule in your team handbook so it is a known policy, not a surprise.

**Step 4: Invite the team by region, in waves.** Start with one region or team as a pilot — ideally the one with the most spending pain. Invite members, assign roles (admin, finance/bookkeeper, employee), and let the pilot run for two weeks. Fix confusing limits and policies before scaling.

**Step 5: Issue virtual cards first.** For each pilot member, issue a personal virtual card. For each shared vendor, issue a vendor card owned by the team lead. Hold physical cards for people who actually travel.

**Step 6: Run a 15-minute onboarding call per timezone band.** One call for the Americas, one for EMEA, one for APAC. Show people three things only: where their card is, what their limit covers, and how to attach a receipt. Record it and drop the recording in the handbook — new hires in any timezone self-serve from there.

**Step 7: Connect accounting before the first month-end.** Sync transactions to your accounting software and map categories while volume is still small. Cleaning up categories after three months of global spend is nobody's idea of fun.

**Step 8: Review after the first cycle.** Look at declined transactions (limits too tight?), out-of-policy flags (policy unclear?) and missing receipts (rule not understood?). Adjust limits and wording, then roll out to the remaining regions.

Done properly, this takes a part-time finance owner about two weeks of elapsed time — most of it waiting for timezones, not working.

## Limits: How Much Can a Global Team Actually Spend?

Your company limit is the ceiling for all card spending; individual card and budget limits divide that ceiling up. Brex calculates the company limit from business financials: connected bank balances, funds in the Brex business account, revenue, capital raised and financial statements. Two practical consequences for distributed teams:

- **Limits move with your finances.** A funding round or a strong revenue quarter can raise capacity; a falling cash balance can lower it. Finance should monitor the limit the way it monitors the bank balance — our [brex card limit](https://brexcardadvisor.com/brex-card-limit) guide explains the mechanics.
- **Distribution is your job, and that's good.** Brex gives the company a ceiling; you decide that engineering's vendor cards get more than the design team's font budget. No bank would know your team well enough to make that call — you do.
